The first motivating question behind this article is whether synthetic algebraic geometry admits a notion similar to the coherent sheaves in classical algebraic geometry. Coherent sheaves share properties with finite-dimensional vector spaces and appear for example in Serre duality, an important tool of classical algebraic geometry.
A proof of Serre duality for a projective scheme $X$ over a field $k$ can be found in \cite{Hartshorne}. To adapt that proof and applications of the theorem to synthetic algebraic geometry, the following facts lead to the first important obstacles:

\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item\label{locally-free-is-coherent} All locally free sheaves are coherent sheaves.
\item\label{coherent-cohomology-groups} For every coherent sheaf $\mathcal F$ on $X$, all cohomology modules $H^i(X,\mathcal F)$ are finite dimensional vector spaces over $k$.
\item\label{dualization-on-k-vector-spaces} Dualization is an exact functor on finite dimensional vector spaces over $k$.
\end{enumerate}

In synthetic algebraic geometry, the base ring $R$ is inherently not a field in a strong enough sense to support these points. Moreover, $R$ is not coherent. Even classically, for non coherent rings, \ref{locally-free-is-coherent} fails.
Over a more general ring, the best one can hope for concerning \ref{coherent-cohomology-groups} is, that the cohomology modules are all in the abelian closure of finite free modules.
Then, \ref{coherent-cohomology-groups} and \ref{dualization-on-k-vector-spaces} together mean that the abelian closure of finite free modules should have an exact dualization functor. Classically, this is very specific to fields and one might think at this point that this proof of Serre duality is not suitable for a synthetic adaption.

However, in~\cite{diffgeo-article}, a surprising observation was made:
Any finitely presented $R$-module $M$ is reflexive, i.e.\ canonically isomorphic to its double dual $M^{\ast\ast}$, where $M^\ast$ is $\Hom_R(M,R)$.
It is known that finitely presented $R$-modules do not form an abelian category, so at least kernels of maps between finitely presented $R$-modules have to be included. We call the modules that merely are such kernels \emph{finitely adequate $R$-modules} and it turns out that they already form an abelian category $\finadeq$ with the following surprisingly good properties:

\begin{itemize}
\item The module $\Hom_R(M, N)$ is finitely adequate when $M$ and $N$ are.
\item Dualization is an equivalence $\Hom_R(\argmhole, R) : (\finadeq)^{\op} \to \finadeq$ and therefore exact.
\item In $\finadeq$, the injective modules are exactly the finitely presented modules and the projective modules are exactly the finitely copresented modules.
\item Finitely adequate $R$-modules are closed under extensions (inside $\Mod{R}$).
\item The category of finitely adequate modules is closed symmetric monoidal, with tensor product the double dual of the usual tensor product.
\end{itemize}

In synthetic algebraic geometry, we work with a generalization of $\mathcal O_X$-module sheaves called $R$-module \emph{bundles}.
Module bundles are just maps $X\to \Mod{R}$, assigning to each point of a type $X$ an $R$-module. In \cite{draft}, the abelian category of \emph{weakly quasi-coherent} $R$-modules was defined and bundles of such modules on schemes were used for cohomological calculations. While these bundles are more general than the usual quasi-coherent $\mathcal O_X$-modules, it was still possible to adapt many proofs from the literature to this setting. Similarly, a synthetic replacement for coherent sheaves should admit the same arguments used in known proofs. However, any direct parallel of the definition of coherent sheaves is bound to fail: classically, this definition only works well if finitely presented modules over the base ring form an abelian category, which is known to be false for $R$.

Our initial hope was that $\mathcal A^\prime$ could be the category of \emph{finitely adequate $R$-modules} and $\mathcal A$ could be the bundles on $X$ with values in $\mathcal A^\prime$. In work in progress based on this article it turned out that $\mathcal A$ has to be a smaller category, for example the category of vector bundles on $X$, in order for the cohomology to take values in finitely adequate $R$-modules. With this restriction it seems to be the case that Serre duality follows with a proof similar to that of Hartshorne. The externalization of this proof would give a constructive proof of Serre duality over a general ring. We do not know of an existing constructive proof or any proof in this generality.

All of the results about the category of finitely adequate modules mentioned above are shown in \Cref{adequate-section}. Prior work in~\cite{diffgeo-article} already showed that finitely copresented $R$-modules are projective, and the argument used differential geometry. The differential geometry of the underlying types of some modules plays an even larger role in the present work and is essential in our proof that finitely adequate $R$-modules are closed under extension.

In \Cref{infinitesimally-linear-section}, as a preparation for the central proofs in \Cref{adequate-section}, we study how the differential geometric properties of the underlying type relate to the algebraic structure of a $R$-module.
The synthetic definition of tangent spaces works for general types. For \emph{infinitesimally linear} types, these tangent spaces are $R$-modules. If $M$ is an infinitesimally linear $R$-module, we can compare $M$ with its own tangent space via a canonical map, which always exists and which we prove to be linear. If, for a general $R$-module, this canonical map turns out to be an equivalence, we call the module \emph{tangential}. We show that tangential $R$-modules are always infinitesimally linear and that the canonical map is an isomorphism to their tangent space at 0.  To our surprise, many $R$-modules in synthetic algebraic geometry are tangential.  In particular, this is true for weakly quasi-coherent $R$-modules and therefore for finitely adequate $R$-modules.

We gave an explicit definition of ``finitely adequate'' above, but the subcategory of finitely adequate $R$-modules has some more conceptual descriptions.
For example, it is the smallest abelian subcategory of $\Mod{R}$ containing all finite free $R$-modules.
More surprisingly, by making use of Adelman's work~\cite{adelman-construction}, one can show that it is in fact the free abelian category on the category of finite free $R$-modules.
For this reason, we considered calling finitely adequate $R$-modules ``Adelman modules'', but decided to name them after the existing notion of adequate modules~\cite[Chapter 46]{stacks-project}, once we were aware of it.

Apart from the free abelian category and slight variations on the definition given in the beginning, we also asked if one of the following categories coincides with the finitely adequate $R$-modules:

\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item Reflexive modules, i.e., all $R$-modules $M$ such that the canonical map with $M \to M^{\ast\ast}$ is an isomorphism.
\item $R$-modules $M$ that are not not finite free.
\end{enumerate}

The answer turned out to be negative in both cases. We present these results in \Cref{examples-section} together with other examples that show the relationships between the various conditions one can put on $R$-modules in synthetic algebraic geometry.
